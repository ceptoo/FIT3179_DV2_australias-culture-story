A data story in six parts

Australia's Culture Story

The languages we speak, the places we protect, where we were born, how we spend our nights out and who pays for it all. Open data from eleven public sources shows how Australia's cultural identity has changed, and what we are at risk of losing.

By Wern Zhong Lei · FIT3179 Data Visualisation 2, Monash University · Published October 2026

Why this story?

Culture is usually described in big words: identity, heritage, community. This page tries to measure it instead. It brings together data from eleven public sources, from the Census and the National Heritage List to library loans and ticket sales, and asks one question: how has Australian culture changed, and who is it changing for?

It is written for any Australian, with no statistics needed. Hover over any chart for exact numbers; some charts also have a menu or a legend you can click.

Throughout the page, terracotta marks Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander languages, places and arts.

01

The first cultures

Long before 1788, this continent held hundreds of languages, each tied to its own Country. Records of 834 of them still carry a location, and many are still spoken today.

Every dot is a language

Languages crowd the coasts and rivers, where most people lived. Queensland alone has 302 recorded languages, more than any other state.

Choose a state under the map to see how many languages were recorded there.

Only languages with a recorded location are shown, so this undercounts the true number.

Being recorded is not the same as being spoken. A language survives when children grow up using it at home.

More Indigenous Australians speak an Indigenous language than ever before, but because the Indigenous population has grown faster, the share who speak one has fallen from one in six to fewer than one in ten. The two most-spoken today are creoles: Yumplatok in the Torres Strait and Kriol across the north.

02

The places we protect

Since 2004, the National Heritage List has named 126 places of outstanding value to the nation. They tell us what Australia has decided is worth keeping.

Mostly historic, mostly south-east

Seventy of the 126 places are historic sites, clustered around Sydney, Melbourne and Hobart. Only 20 are listed for their Indigenous values.

Budj Bim in Victoria, one of the first places listed in 2004, holds eel traps built by the Gunditjmara people that are thousands of years old.

Click a type in the legend to show only those places. Green areas are World Heritage properties.

03

Who we became

In 1901, almost one in five Australians had been born in Britain or Ireland. Today, people born in India, China and the Philippines are reshaping who Australians are.

Three waves

Shrinking: from 1901 to 1947 few new migrants arrived, and the share born overseas fell to under 10%.

Europe: after the Second World War, Italians, Greeks, Dutch and Yugoslavs arrived in large numbers.

Asia: once the White Australia policy ended in 1973, migration from Asia grew, and by 2021 India was the second-largest birthplace after the UK.

04

How we take part

A culture is also what people do with their weekends. The national attendance survey of 2021–22 ran during COVID-19 restrictions, so it captures a culture on pause.

Every state lost audiences, but Victoria, with the longest lockdowns, lost the most. Then people came back.

By 2025, 74% of Australians had been to a live arts event or festival in the past year, up from 68% in 2022. Attendance at First Nations arts grew too, to one in three Australians. The biggest gains came from people aged 55 and over.

05

Libraries and the stage

Two very different cultural spaces, one free and one ticketed, both hit hard by COVID-19 and both back above where they were before it.

Public libraries lent 174 million items in 2023–24, the most in five years, and almost a third of them were digital. Each Australian now borrows more, yet spending per person has fallen by 12% after inflation.

A two-city industry

Live performance collapsed in 2020, then came back bigger than before: a record 31.4 million tickets in 2024, almost half of them for contemporary music.

New South Wales and Victoria sold almost two in every three tickets. Victorians bought the most per person: 1.43 tickets each.

Tickets are counted where the show was held, so interstate visitors to Sydney and Melbourne raise those states' numbers.

06

Who pays for culture

Australian governments spent $8.6 billion on culture in 2023–24, 49% more than in 2015–16 before inflation. Where that money comes from, and where it goes, differs sharply between states.

The Northern Territory and South Australia spend the most per person, more than $300 each, while Queensland spends the least. Nationally, the largest single item is the Australian Government's $1.6 billion for radio and television, mostly the ABC and SBS. Local councils add more than $2 billion a year, including most public library funding.

—

So, who are we now?

Put the six threads together and a picture of modern Australia appears.

  • The oldest cultures are still here

    Almost 77,000 people speak an Indigenous language at home, more than ever. But only 139 languages are being passed to children, and Indigenous places are a small part of what we protect.

  • We are a nation of migrants

    Nearly one in three Australians was born overseas, and the main birthplaces have moved from Britain to Europe to Asia in just over a century.

  • We came back stronger, on thinner budgets

    Ticket sales and library loans are now above pre-COVID levels and arts attendance keeps rising, while real spending on libraries per person has fallen.

Australia's cultural identity is not fixed. It is being rewritten by each new arrival, and kept alive by the communities who still speak the first languages. The data shows both a richer culture than in 1901 and a fragile one.

About this visualisation

Who it is for: any Australian curious about how the nation's culture has changed. No statistical knowledge is needed.

How to read it: hover over any mark for exact values. The language map has a state menu, the heritage map's legend can be clicked to filter, and two charts have menus to change what they show.

Things to know: survey results (sections 04 and 06) are estimates from samples. Money is in dollars of the day unless marked "real". Country-of-birth figures from 2001 are population estimates rather than Census counts. The 2021 Census asked about languages "used" rather than "spoken" at home.
