01

The first cultures

Hundreds of languages were spoken here long before 1788, and whether they survive now depends on children learning them.

Every dot is a language

  • Densest along northern coasts and rivers.
  • Queensland has the most: 302.
  • Pick a state below the map.

Only languages with a known location.

But a recorded language is not always a spoken one. A language survives only if children grow up using it at home.

The number of speakers keeps rising because the Indigenous population is growing, yet the share who speak a language at home keeps falling. The two most-spoken today, Yumplatok and Kriol, are creoles that formed after colonisation.

02

The places we protect

Since 2004 the nation has listed 126 heritage places. Most are historic sites; only 20 are listed for their Indigenous values.

Mostly historic, mostly south-east

  • 70 historic, 36 natural, 20 Indigenous.
  • Historic sites crowd the south-east.
  • Budj Bim: ancient Gunditjmara eel traps.

Use "Show" below the map to filter.

03

Who we became

Migration has reshaped who Australians are: first from Britain and Ireland, then from Europe, and since the 1970s from Asia.

Three waves

  • 1901–1947: few arrivals; share falls to 10%.
  • Post-war: Italians, Greeks, Dutch arrive.
  • After 1973: migration from Asia grows.

04

How we take part

COVID-19 emptied cultural venues in 2021–22. A separate survey shows that arts attendance has kept growing since 2022.

Every state lost audiences, and Victoria, which had the longest lockdowns, lost the most.

Between 2022 and 2025, live arts attendance rose from 68% to 74%, and First Nations arts now reach one in three Australians. The biggest gains came from people aged 55 and over, who had attended least in 2022.

A different survey from the COVID charts above, so the two are not compared.

05

Libraries and the stage

Library loans and ticket sales are now above 2019–20 levels, even though library spending per person has fallen after inflation.

Australians borrowed 174 million library items in 2023–24, almost a third of them digital. Loans per person rose 18% while real spending per person fell 12%, so libraries are doing more with less.

A two-city industry

  • Record 31.4 million tickets in 2024.
  • NSW and Victoria: two in three tickets.
  • Victorians bought most per person: 1.43.

Counted where the show was held.

06

Who pays for culture

Governments spent $8.6 billion on culture in 2023–24, 49% more than in 2015–16 before inflation, but spending per person varies widely between states.

Per person, the NT leads

  • NT and SA: over $300 each.
  • Queensland spends least: $173.

Federal spending is not split by state.

The Australian Government’s biggest single item is $1.6 billion for radio and television. Councils added $2.2 billion in 2023–24, more than any single category.

—

So, who are we now?

Taken together, the six chapters show a culture that is becoming more diverse while its oldest parts remain at risk.

  • The oldest cultures are still here

    About 77,000 people speak an Indigenous language at home, but only 139 languages are being passed to children.

  • We are a nation of migrants

    Nearly one in three Australians was born overseas in 2021, up from one in ten in 1947.

  • We came back stronger, on thinner budgets

    Ticket sales hit a record and library loans a five-year high, while real library spending per person fell.

Australia’s culture is richer and more varied than in 1901, but its first languages will only survive if children keep learning them.

About this visualisation

Who it is for: any Australian. No statistics needed.

How to read it: hover for exact values; menus and buttons filter some charts.

Things to know: survey results are estimates. Money is not inflation-adjusted unless marked "real". Birthplace data from 2001 are population estimates.

Colour: terracotta always means Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander data. Each chapter has its own colour family. On maps and the heatmap, darker means more.
